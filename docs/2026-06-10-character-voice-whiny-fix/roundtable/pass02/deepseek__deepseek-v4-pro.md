## Blind Spots

### 1. The zero‑shot cloner may strip lower formants irrespective of the reference
The audition‑reel methodology (P2b) tests the *output* of the cloner, which is good, but it doesn’t ask whether the model routinely attenuates the 100–400 Hz region *even from a full‑bodied reference*. If IndexTTS2’s decoder or the mel‑spectrogram inversion has a bias toward a brighter, more “telephone” bandwidth, even the best reference will produce a thin voice. The chest‑weight measurement in P2a’s audit runs on the *reference* file, not the cloned output.  
**UNSURE** internal: A separate measurement on cloned neutral‑text output (e.g., spectrum of a “the quick brown fox” sentence) would isolate a model‑intrinsic thinning. This is not equivalent to the solo‑vs‑mix gate because that gate only compares loudness; spectral tilt could be the culprit.

### 2. The emotion‑vector’s influence on vocal fry / breathiness is uncharted
Whiny voices often combine a high‑rising terminal contour with vocal fry or excessive breathiness. The plan maps keywords → emotion dimensions, but the *acoustic realisation* of e.g. “sad” or “afraid” in IndexTTS2 might add creak or aspirated voice quality that listeners perceive as pleading. The audition matrix includes vector‑omitted baseline and pure‑calm cell, but none that tests “angry = 0.3, all others zero” or “melancholic = 0.5” in isolation to see if a single dimension triggers a whiny artifact (G‑14, G‑7, G‑8). The emotion‑axis rotation experiment (appendix) would probe this, but it’s optional. A more direct blind‑spot test: render a line with the same reference, vector = {surprised:0.8}, and listen for pitch/acoustic quality vs. a vector‑off line – does it become thinner or just higher pitched?

### 3. Punctuation may drive prosody even when stripped
The punctuation lever replaces `?` with `.`, but IndexTTS2 might still infer a questioning contour from the lexical content (“what”, “how”) even without the symbol, because the model’s internal prosody predictor may use keyword cues much like the delivery vector does (G‑12, G‑13). The plan’s condition 6 (vector‑omitted, softened punctuation) isolates punctuation, but not the model’s independent keyword‑to‑prosody mapping. A stronger test would be to rewrite a heavily interrogative line *without* question words (e.g., replace “What do you see?” with “Describe the object.”) and compare the intonation.

### 4. Ref sample‑rate mismatch may introduce aliasing that trims top‑end (not just the rumoured low‑end)
The reference bank is 44100 Hz peak‑normalised (G‑14). The engine expects 22050 Hz (G‑14). If IndexTTS2’s internal resampling uses a low‑quality filter or no anti‑aliasing, the prompt’s high‑frequency energy could fold down, creating an odd nasal/metallic sheen that gets cloned. P0 cell 9 tests a *pre‑resampled* ref, but that only checks whether the cloner sounds better with a clean 22050 Hz prompt – it doesn’t guarantee that the *default resampling path* is the culprit. If cell 9 sounds markedly fuller, the blind spot is not “resample unclean” but that you might need to pre‑resample *all* refs as a versioned asset change.

### 5. The emotional “calm” floor in the current vector (G‑6) may be acting as an *anti‑vibrato* suppressor
The plan notes calm semantics are unverified and wisely removes the floor (P1.1), but **UNSURE** that `calm=0` to IndexTTS2 means “neutral” rather than “unconstrained expressiveness.” A zero‑calm vector might allow the model’s natural vibrato or dynamic range, which could actually *reduce* the perception of a constrained, whiny delivery. The opposite is also possible: a tiny `calm` value could suppress excitation and make the voice sound “held back.” The plan already tests a pure‑calm=1.0 cell (P0 cell 4), but no cell explores a vector where all dimensions are *zero* (the true‑neutral render from G‑8). That zero‑vector render is a critical missing diagnostic: is the “whine” present even without any explicit emotion? If yes, the problem is firmly in the reference/model; if not, the vector is the dominant lever.

### 6. The caster’s uniform random pick (G‑4) can amplify rare biases
A single poor male reference in the pool of 14 will be picked with probability 1/14 every time the ladder reaches that tier. Because the ladder prefers exact matches, a character that matches only the gender tier will pick *any* male ref uniformly, without any quality bias. The tier pre‑filter (P2c) will eventually remove bad refs, but until then, the **blind spot** is that the plan’s P0 audition matrix chooses specific refs – it doesn’t simulate the lottery’s stochastic exposure. Without P3a observability, the operator may simply never see a character land on the worst ref.

## Craft – What a Voice Director or Post Engineer Would Do

### (A) Pre‑render reference “dressing” as a single automatable step
A post engineer receiving a “thin” reference from a voice‑over session would immediately apply **EQ and gentle saturation** before giving it to the talent copycat. Automatable: for every reference in the bank, compute a smooth spectral tilt compensation (e.g., a fixed +4 dB low‑shelf below 300 Hz, –2 dB around 2.5 kHz to reduce nasal resonance), but only for clones flagged as “nasal_risk” or “thin” from the audit. This is similar to the experiment‑appendix “ref transforms” but can be parameterised and baked as a versioned ref‑generation tool (non‑destructive to originals). The same bias could be applied to the *rendered output* of the character voice if a post‑processing unit is allowed in the pipeline, but that would change the audio‑spine contract (see invariants). So **apply it to the clone’s prompt** – the model will clone the EQ’d timbre. This leverages the cloner’s own behaviour as a “re‑recording” chain.

### (B) Use a fixed‑length “vocal weight” guide tone mixed with the reference
A voice director might ask the actor to “speak from your chest” by doing a low‑hum exercise. Mechanically: generate a short, synthetic low‑frequency drone (∼100 Hz, soft amplitude) and mix it subtly into the reference prompt *before* cloning. The cloner, if it captures harmonic structure, might copy the spectral envelope and add weight. This is speculative but easy to test: take a thin ref, add a 100 Hz sine at −30 dB relative to peak, and clone a neutral line. Compare to the untreated ref output. If it works, you can script a per‑ref “weight injection” version.

### (C) Character‑specific dynamic loudness‑matching that preserves “acting” dynamics but lifts average level
Post‑production for dialogue in a music bed wouldn’t just RMS‑match a single clip; they’d run **light compression (2‑3 dB) with a slow attack and a gentle low‑frequency expander** to give the voice presence without flattening it. Automatable: after rendering the character line, apply an EQ+compressor chain that’s part of the voice engine’s post‑process, versioned and gated by an env flag (e.g., `OTR_CHAR_POST=1`). This is cheap, fully deterministic (fixed compressor settings), and directly addresses the H2c channel *before* the mix. The plan’s P4 solo‑vs‑mix gate could trigger enabling this post‑process as a configuration change, keeping the byte‑identical contract because the post‑process is part of the voice node’s output.

### (D) Test “breath control” by trimming breathy tails from the reference
In narration‑style references, clip endings often have a soft exhale or a breath which, when cloned, can cause the synthetic voice to adopt a breathy, “lacking projection” quality. The audit should measure the RMS of the last 200 ms vs. the body and flag references with a high end‑to‑body breath ratio. Automatable: trim the last 0.3 s from the reference clip (if it’s silence/breath) before cloning, leaving only the core spoken content. This eliminates a potential “whiny tail” learned by the model.

## Critique of the Plan (Sections 5‑6)

### P0‑zero – Engine verification: insufficient for past episodes
Agree it’s essential, but it only resolves uncertainty for fresh renders. Until P3a ships, the diagnostic gap remains for already‑produced content. The plan acknowledges this; no action needed, just an explicit note that the operator’s judgment of “whiny” may be influenced by those older bark‑rendered episodes.

### P0 audition matrix – good coverage, but a missing condition: true‑neutral (all‑zero vector)
As noted in blind spot 5, the matrix includes `vector omitted` (which defaults to `calm=1.0` in the current path) but not the **true all‑zero vector** that results from `OTR_DELIVERY_VECTOR=0`. Adding that as condition `0` (or after condition 1) is cheap and could immediately show whether the whine is completely vector‑driven.

### P1.1 – switching derivation to prepared text: risk of removing stage directions that convey affect
When switching to prepared text, parentheticals (e.g., “(terrified)”) are stripped. The original raw‑text derivation (G13) could pick up `terrified` from the parenthetical, giving an appropriate afraid cue. Stripping it might make the line sound calmer than intended, possibly solving the “whiny” over‑expression but losing intentional dramatic direction. A safer interim: extract cues from both raw and prepared text, but weight cues that survive cleaning higher, and demote those only in raw text. However, the preparation strips them entirely. This is a trade‑off; the plan’s P0 will reveal if it helps. **Order mis‑ordering risk**: if P0 shows that the vector is the dominant source of whine, the delivery table retune might be over‑engineered; simply lowering alpha could be sufficient.

### P1.3 – punctuation lever default off: the vector derivation still sees the question
The lever replaces terminal `?` with `.` after vector derivation, so the vector will still cue surprised/afraid from `?`. That’s deliberate, but it means that even with the lever on, a line full of `?` will send a strong emotion vector to the engine. If the engine’s prosody from punctuation is the main source of pleading (H2b), turning on the lever might not fully remove the whine because the vector still pushes the voice into an afraid/surprised timbre. The lever would be stronger if it *also* neutralised the vector for the affected dimensions. This could be a follow‑up configuration.

### P2c – tier pre‑filtering + P2d – reuse off: without P3a, you can’t verify who gets which ref
The tier pre‑filter reduces the pool. With reuse off, some characters may get no casting and fall back to render‑time `_resolve_clone_ref_path`. That fallback currently does **not** use tier filtering (it calls `assign_voice_for_slot` with the full bank or gender‑agnostic pick). So a character that fails casting could still end up with a tier‑“reject” voice. The plan must ensure that the render‑time fallback also respects the bank’s `quality_tier` field, or at minimum that `_resolve_clone_ref_path` passes a pre‑filtered bank. The current plan describes “tier logic = pool pre‑filter before the ladder, never reweight _pick” but only in the context of the caster; it doesn’t mention modifying `_resolve_clone_ref_path`. Without that, tier‑“reject” refs can leak through.

### P3a – additive ledger persistence: verify durable‑ledger route early
The plan says to verify the video fallback’s restamping route; if that route is specific to the video node’s internal structure, an alternative path (e.g., writing a parallel JSON sidecar) may be necessary. This is a build‑time dependency that could delay the coder. Recommend: before starting the coder session, have the operator (or a quick staging script) confirm that `render_log` strings are enough and the durable ledger update can be done via the same mechanism as `OTR_CastLock` uses to stamp `voice_ref_id`. The plan’s “additive fields only” is safe.

### P4 – solo‑vs‑mix gate: this should be performed BEFORE committing to a fix, not after
Listening to the isolated character lines early (during P0 or immediately after) would tell whether the problem is solely in the mix. The plan schedules it as a verification gate. To avoid wasted tuning, run a quick solo‑vs‑mix check with the existing character lines (before any changes) – if solo sounds fine, the work tilts toward a mix‑lane solution.

## Wildcards (30‑minute experiments)

1. **“Glottal pulse synthesis” on the reference clip.** Use a low‑frequency pulse (e.g., a smoothed 100 Hz square wave) blended at a very low level (−35 dB relative to peak) into the reference audio before cloning. The cloner may copy this low‑frequency presence as part of the voice timbre, adding perceived chest resonance without altering the original clip’s professional sound. This is not a typical audio processing trick, but zero‑shot models can be surprisingly sensitive to low‑level spectral cues. If it works, it can be automated as a per‑ref pre‑processing step for those tagged “thin”.

2. **Emotion‑vector “bleed” from the announcer’s delivery.** The announcer (Kokoro) is perceived as fine. One wild experiment: after rendering the character line, extract its vocal energy envelope and the announcer’s envelope from the same timeframe (if available), then apply a subtle amplitude modulation to the character clip that matches the announcer’s macro‑prosody (slower attack, more sustained body). This is a crude “style transfer” that doesn’t change pitch but could alter perceived confidence. Might require alignment, but a sliding‑window RMS matching could work. Speculation; likely too advanced.

3. **“Forced low‑formant” via linear prediction (LPC) shift on the cloned output.** After synthesis, analyse the voice with LPC, increase the bandwidth of the first formant slightly (or shift all formants down by a factor), and resynthesise. This is the post‑equivalent of pitch‑shifting a formant‑aware way, which can add weight without changing perceived gender excessively. Easily scriptable with `librosa` or `pysptk`. If it fixes the whine without artefacts, it could be a versioned post‑process flag. This is similar to the pitch‑shifting experiment in the appendix but operates on the formant structure directly.
